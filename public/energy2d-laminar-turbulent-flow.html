Laminar vs. Turbulent Flow

A thread of smoke rises straight and smooth, then suddenly dissolves into swirling chaos. That breakdown is one of the deepest puzzles in physics.

By Charles Xie ✉ · March 12, 2026

Watch smoke curl off a candle, or cream poured into coffee, or steam off a cup. For a moment the flow is glassy and orderly — then it trips, folds over on itself, and breaks into a tangle of swirls. You have just seen the transition from laminar to turbulent flow, and it is happening all around you: in the air over an airplane wing, the water in a pipe, the blood in your arteries, and the weather over your head.

The simulation below lets you trigger that transition on demand. Press Run and compare the four channels.

What you're watching

Four channels are stacked one above the other. Each has the same warm source on the left, and a blower that pushes air past it — but the wind speed rises from the top channel to the bottom. In the slowest channel, heat is carried downstream as a clean, smooth ribbon: this is laminar flow, where the fluid moves in tidy parallel layers that glide past one another without mixing. As the speed climbs in the lower channels, that ribbon starts to ripple, then to curl, and finally to shatter into turbulent eddies that fling warm fluid up, down, and across the channel. The thermometers and the built-in plots of temperature, flow speed, and heat flux let you measure the difference rather than just admire it — same heat source, very different mixing.

The Reynolds number

Whether a flow stays smooth or turns chaotic comes down to a tug-of-war between two forces: inertia, which carries the fluid forward and amplifies any little wobble, and viscosity, the internal friction that smooths wobbles out. The ratio of the two is captured in a single famous quantity, the Reynolds number. When it is low — slow speeds, thick fluids, narrow gaps — viscosity wins and the flow is laminar. When it is high — fast speeds, thin fluids, wide spaces — inertia wins and the flow goes turbulent. That is exactly the knob the simulation turns: raising the wind speed down the channels raises the Reynolds number until smooth flow can no longer hold together, which is why the note in the model reads "stronger wind is more likely to cause turbulent flow."

Turbulence itself remains one of the great unsolved problems of classical physics. The equations that govern fluids, the Navier–Stokes equations, are easy to write down and notoriously hard to solve once the swirls set in — so much so that a million-dollar prize still stands for proving some of their basic properties.

Why it matters

The laminar–turbulent line is one of the most consequential in engineering, and the catch is that neither side is simply "better." Turbulence dramatically increases drag, so aircraft and car designers fight to keep flow laminar over wings and bodies to save fuel. But turbulence also mixes far more effectively, so the same engineers want it inside heat exchangers, combustion chambers, and chemical reactors, where rapid mixing means faster heat transfer and more complete reactions. Knowing where the transition happens — and being able to nudge it — is the whole game.
