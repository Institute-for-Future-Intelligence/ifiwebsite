Why a Quantum Dot's Color Is a Matter of Size

The same material glows blue, green, or red depending only on how small its crystals are: ask an AI agent to find the size that makes a television's green pixel, without cadmium.

← Back to AIMS

By Charles Xie ✉

If you make crystals of indium phosphide small enough, their color depends on how small they are. Crystals under 2 nm across fluoresce blue under an ultraviolet lamp. At around 2.5 nm the same material fluoresces green, at 3 nm orange, and by 4 nm red. Semiconductor crystals this small are called quantum dots, and this size effect is a common example in a nanoscience course. Alexei Ekimov discovered it in the early 1980s while studying glass colored with copper chloride: heating the glass longer grew larger crystals in it, and the color of the glass moved with them. A few years later Louis Brus saw the same thing in cadmium sulfide particles in solution and explained it. Moungi Bawendi later developed a way to grow such particles to a chosen size in quantity, which is what made them useful. The three shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The effect has been commercialized. A quantum-dot television puts a film of dots of two sizes between its blue backlight and the screen. Some of the blue passes through; the rest is absorbed by the dots and re-emitted as red and green. How pure those two colors are depends in part on how tightly the factory can hold the size of the dots.

Textbooks show the effect with a photograph of vials glowing from blue to red and explain it with quantum confinement, but a student reading about it cannot try it. In AIMS they can. They ask the AI agent a question, and the agent cuts the dots out of the crystal, computes the energy levels of each, and shows the result in the chat as a chart. No color is preset. Everything comes from the positions of a few hundred atoms and the quantum mechanics of their electrons. In the window below, the agent has been asked a question that a display engineer might actually face, and has answered it over four turns.

Why a Smaller Box Glows Bluer

The dots in this article are tiny crystals of a semiconductor, and their color follows from how a semiconductor emits light: an electron falls from the conduction band into the valence band, and the energy of the photon is the band gap. For indium phosphide the gap is 1.35 eV, which is in the infrared. In a crystal only a few nanometers across the picture changes. In quantum mechanics the electron behaves as a wave, and a wave confined to a smaller box must bend more sharply, which costs kinetic energy. Every level rises and the gap widens. This is the effect of quantum confinement. The extra energy grows roughly as one over the diameter squared, the particle-in-a-box result from an introductory course in quantum mechanics (in real dots, and in the model below, the power is nearer 1.2 than 2): smaller dot, bluer light. Since the bands of a dot have become a ladder of discrete levels, AIMS reports a HOMO–LUMO gap, the spacing between the highest occupied and the lowest unoccupied level, as it would for a molecule. Light can excite an electron across larger gaps too, which is what the dot's absorption spectrum shows, but an excited electron drops to the lowest empty level within a picosecond before it emits, so the color of the glow is set by the smallest gap.

Going from a gap to a color takes one division: the wavelength in nanometers is 1240 divided by the energy in electron volts. For bulk indium phosphide that gives 918 nm. For 530 nm, the green a display wants, the gap has to be 2.34 eV. Ask the agent to "build a 2 nm indium phosphide quantum dot and compute its energy levels" and it carves a sphere of 147 indium and phosphorus atoms out of the crystal, caps the surface, solves for the levels, and reports 2.57 eV, or 482 nm, which is blue. Confinement can only widen the gap, so a dot can be bluer than the crystal it came from but never redder, and that constraint matters below.

The Design Problem: A Green Pixel Without Cadmium

The first quantum dots to reach the market were cadmium selenide, and cadmium is restricted in consumer electronics. The industry switched to indium phosphide. But indium phosphide is not the only cadmium-free semiconductor, and a display engineer worries about more than hitting a color once. No synthesis makes every dot the same size. If the diameters in a batch vary by 5%, the batch emits a band of wavelengths, and the band is as wide as the slope of color against size times the spread. The faster a material's color changes with size, the harder it is to make dots that emit the same green batch after batch.

So the first question in the example asks for more than a color: "Design a green quantum dot for a display without cadmium: compare indium phosphide, zinc telluride and zinc selenide dots, pick the material that reaches 530 nm with the least color shift per nanometer of diameter, and build it." The agent adds cadmium telluride, a cadmium compound of the kind the industry is leaving, as a point of comparison. Then, in one tool call, it cuts dots of all four materials over a range of sizes, solves them, plots the four curves on one chart, and reads off where each crosses 530 nm and how steeply.

Indium phosphide is the answer, and the table says something the usual one-line summary ("indium phosphide replaced cadmium") leaves out: the switch cost almost nothing in tolerance. Cadmium telluride hits green at the same size with about the same slope, so a 5% spread in size smears the peak of either material by roughly 6 nm each way. That is small next to the 35 to 40 nm linewidth of commercial indium phosphide dots, most of which comes from the dots themselves rather than from their spread in size. Where the slope matters is in hitting the color at all: at 105 nm per nm, a batch whose dots average 0.05 nm too large emits 5 nm too red, and 0.05 nm is a fraction of one atomic layer. (The slopes come from a power-law fit through the computed dots near the target. An early version of the tool used the raw slope between neighboring dots, which made indium phosphide look twice as steep as cadmium telluride only because of where the discrete atom cuts happened to fall.)

The Rule That Falls Out

The two zinc compounds fail for opposite reasons, and between them they give the rule for picking a host. Zinc selenide is blue already as a bulk crystal, at 459 nm. Confinement can only push it further into the blue, so no zinc selenide dot of any size emits green. The agent reports the target as unreachable rather than extrapolating.

Zinc telluride fails the other way. Its bulk emission is 549 nm, almost green, so getting to 530 nm takes hardly any confinement. That means a large dot, and on a large dot the color barely moves with size, which is just what a manufacturer wants. But the dot would have to be bigger than the model can handle. The trend of the computed points puts it at 15 nm or more, where zinc telluride is for practical purposes bulk material, and the agent says so: the slope is too far out to trust. The second question in the example asks why zinc telluride would be steadiest if it could be made, and the answer is a rule: the host whose bulk gap lies just below the target color gives the steadiest dot, because it needs the least confinement, at the cost of a dot too big to be useful. Indium phosphide sits between the two zinc compounds: far enough below green that a small dot reaches it, close enough that the slope at the crossing is moderate.

Does the Shape Matter?

Real dots are not all spheres, so the third question asks whether a 2.4 nm indium phosphide cube emits the same green as the 2.4 nm sphere. It does not. The cube comes out 18 nm bluer, 513 nm against 531 nm, and one might conclude that shape matters.

The atom counts, which the agent prints beside every structure, explain the difference. The 2.4 nm sphere has 281 indium and phosphorus atoms; the 2.4 nm cube has 239. Cutting a cube from a crystal leaves more loose atoms at the corners, and the cleanup step that removes singly bonded surface atoms takes them away, something the nominal size does not record. Put 239 atoms into a sphere at the crystal's density and the sphere is 2.25 nm across. The cube is really a 2.25 nm dot, and a 2.25 nm dot is 18 nm bluer than a 2.4 nm one.

The fourth question asks for the fair comparison: spheres and cubes swept together from 1.2 to 2.6 nm and plotted at their effective diameter, the diameter of the sphere their atoms would fill. The two curves fall on top of each other. At equal atom count the shape hardly matters. For anyone making dots, the size distribution is the thing to control; shape is secondary. And a nominal size is a label, not a measurement.

What Is Under the Hood, and What to Trust

Each dot in the example is an actual cluster of atoms, cut from the crystal and solved with the empirical tight-binding model that AIMS uses for bulk band structures. Each atom has five orbitals, and each bond couples neighboring atoms with parameters fitted to the measured gaps and critical points of the bulk crystal. Dangling bonds at the surface are capped, as a hydrogen shell would cap a real dot, by pushing their states out of the gap. The levels then come from diagonalizing the whole matrix, which is practical up to about 1600 orbitals, or about 320 host atoms. That is why the sweep stops near 2.6 nm, and why the agent says so when a design asks for a bigger dot.

This is a textbook model, not a research one, and every card in the example says as much. It omits the exciton, the attraction between the excited electron and the hole it left, worth about a tenth of an electron volt at these sizes. It omits spin–orbit coupling, which matters for the heavier elements. And its nearest-neighbor parameters make the electrons too heavy, so the confinement shifts come out a little low. These errors are the same for every host and every shape, so the comparisons survive them even though the absolute numbers do not. A real 2.4 nm indium phosphide dot should emit within a few tens of nanometers of the predicted 531 nm, not at it. Part of the reason is that the model predicts where a dot absorbs, and real dots emit somewhat to the red of where they absorb (the Stokes shift), so a green emitter is a bit smaller than 2.4 nm; the green dots in a real display are also core–shell particles, and the shell shifts the color too. Each card prints the bulk gap beside the dot's gap because that one number is fitted to experiment.

Nothing Here Was Programmed

The model is given only the atoms and a handful of fitted parameters for their electrons. Everything below emerged from them:

  • Blue to green — a 2 nm indium phosphide dot comes out at 482 nm and a 2.4 nm dot at 531 nm; the color-versus-size curve is an output of the calculation, not an input.
  • The sizing curve — the 1/dp confinement law, with p near 1.2 for these dots, is fitted to the computed points rather than assumed.
  • The one-way rule — zinc selenide cannot reach green at any size, because confinement only widens the gap.
  • The host rule — the material whose bulk gap sits just below the target gives the steadiest color, and the steadiest candidate of all needs a dot too large to be practical.
  • Size over shape — a cube and a sphere with the same number of atoms emit the same green; the "2.4 nm cube" was bluer only because it held fewer atoms.

Conclusion

The point of this article is less the quantum dot than the way of studying it. A student asks a design question in plain language, and an AI agent turns it into a quantum mechanical calculation on actual atoms and reports what it finds. The student ends up knowing what size of indium phosphide dot emits green, why zinc selenide never will, why zinc telluride would be ideal if it could be made, and why a 2.4 nm cube emits the color of a smaller sphere. None of it was looked up in a reference. It was computed in front of them, from the atoms up, which is what AIMS is for.
