Quantitative Structure–Property Relationships of Cycloalkanes

Cycloalkanes form a monocyclic hydrocarbon family whose boiling points rise with carbon count — another clean QSPR to build and validate in AIMS.

By Charles Xie ✉ · Updated July 11, 2026

Cycloalkanes are a family of hydrocarbon molecules whose carbon atoms form a single ring (i.e., monocyclic). As with the linear alkanes, their boiling points increase with the number of carbon atoms. With AIMS, you can study this quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR).

The graph in the window above plots the boiling points of the cycloalkanes against their heavy atom counts (i.e., the number of carbon atoms). You can fit a regression model to these data and test it in the same way as described in the article on linear alkanes.

QSPR modeling gives us a tool to analyze the data, but it does not provide any explanation about the result by itself. To make sense of the result, we still need to resort to fundamentals in chemistry. Equipped with the power of molecular dynamics simulations, AIMS allows us to design and conduct computational experiments to check a QSPR model.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

The following two simulations allow you to compare the boiling points of cyclopropane and cyclodecane on a qualitative basis. The dashed lines represent the van der Waals interactions among the atoms.

Cyclopropane (C3H6)

There are 22 cyclopropane molecules (198 atoms in total) in this simulation. The temperature is initially set to be 300 K (27°C or 81°F). A greatly exaggerated gravitational field is applied to keep the molecules at the bottom of the container when they condense. As you can see, at this temperature, cyclopropane is in the gaseous state.

Cyclodecane (C10H20)

There are seven cyclodecane molecules (210 atoms in total) in this simulation. The temperature is initially set to be 300 K (27°C or 81°F). As with cyclopropane above, a greatly exaggerated gravitational field is applied to keep the molecules near the bottom of the container when they condense. As you can see, at this temperature, the cyclodecane molecules are in a condensed state occupying the lower part of the container.

These simulations do not predict the boiling points of cyclopropane and cyclodecane (−33°C and 201°C in experiments, respectively). What they show is that, at 300 K, cyclopropane is a gas and cyclodecane is condensed, which places the boiling point of cyclopropane below 300 K and that of cyclodecane above it, in agreement with the experimental data. With only a handful of molecules and an exaggerated gravitational field, the picture is qualitative. Cyclodecane has the higher boiling point because its larger rings have more contact area with one another and therefore stronger total van der Waals attraction to keep them together, as indicated by the dashed lines shown in the above windows.

Cyclodecane also boils at a higher temperature than decane (174°C), its linear counterpart with the same number of carbon atoms. Can you explain why?
