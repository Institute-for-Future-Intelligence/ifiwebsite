Molecular Crowding with Gay–Berne Particles

AIMS crowds suspensions of rods and of hinged chains until they order, and an AI agent runs the experiments.

← Back to AIMS

By Charles Xie ✉ · Updated November 10, 2026

The inside of a cell is crowded. Proteins, RNA, ribosomes, and filaments take up a fifth to a third of its volume, a figure Ellis called obvious but underappreciated in 2001, and Zimmerman and Trach estimated the protein and RNA in the cytoplasm of E. coli at several hundred grams per liter. Biochemists call the consequences macromolecular crowding. A molecule in a crowd has less room than it would in a dilute solution, and the arrangements that leave the crowd the most room win: compact shapes over open ones, complexes over free parts, and, for long thin objects, alignment over tumbling. Asakura and Oosawa showed in 1954 that two large bodies in a solution of smaller ones are pushed together because the small ones gain room when the two touch, and Minton and Zhou, Rivas and Minton reviewed how the same effect shifts folding, binding, and assembly inside a cell. The effect is entropic: the temperature stays the same, the forces between the molecules stay the same, and the order comes from the room that is left.

The simplest case of it was worked out by Onsager in 1949 for long thin rods that do nothing to one another except refuse to overlap. A rod in a dilute suspension points wherever it likes. Pack enough rods together and a rod that lies across its neighbors is boxed in, while one that lies along them can still slide about; above a certain concentration the room gained by aligning outweighs the freedom of pointing anywhere, and the suspension turns nematic at any temperature. Suspensions of tobacco mosaic virus were the first biological liquid crystals seen: Bawden, Pirie, Bernal and Fankuchen reported in 1936 that the concentrated sap of infected plants separates into a birefringent layer, Fraden, Maret, Caspar and Meyer measured the transition in 1989, and Dogic and Fraden found the same ordering in the rod-like fd virus. This article follows two AIMS examples that reproduce the effect in a browser. In the first, two hundred virus-like rods are squeezed until they line up and then expanded until they tumble again. In the second, the rods are joined in hinged pairs, the shortest chain that can bend, and the question becomes the one Flory asked in 1956 about polymers: when a chain is crowded, does its stiffness decide whether it orders? In both, a student asks a plain-language question and an AI agent sets up the cell, runs the molecular dynamics, measures the order, and posts the chart in the conversation.

A Rod That Only Takes Up Room

The rod in both examples is the Gay–Berne particle of 1981, an ellipsoid whose attraction for a neighbor depends on how the two are oriented. It was made for liquid crystals, where one ellipsoid stands for one molecule and the attraction between side-by-side neighbors makes the nematic as the liquid is cooled. Crowding asks the opposite of it. The attraction is turned down to 20 K, a fifteenth of the thermal energy at room temperature, so that the only thing a rod does to a neighbor is keep it out of the room it occupies, and whatever order appears comes from shape and concentration alone. This is coarse-grained modeling: a tobacco mosaic virus is 2130 protein subunits around a strand of RNA, and the model keeps the one fact about it that decides whether a suspension of it orders, that it is a rod about seventeen times longer than it is wide. The rods here are 40 Å long and 4 Å wide, an aspect ratio of 10, so they are stubby viruses 75 times shorter and 45 times thinner than the real one. For hard rods only the aspect ratio and the concentration matter, which is why Onsager's theory applies to the virus and to these rods alike. The rods are made light so that they turn quickly, and the lab works in real units: the thermometer reads kelvin, the barostat reads megapascals, and the concentrations below can be compared with the ones in a cell. A companion article, Coarse-Grained Modeling of Biology, uses the same rod for a lipid bilayer and for filaments aligned by a narrow cell.

Running Experiments by Asking

AIMS is an AI-native environment. Squeezing a suspension with a barostat, bringing a cell to a chosen volume fraction and holding it there, measuring an order parameter or a persistence length, and plotting it are the agent's work. A student asks "do they order by crowding?", and the agent recognizes the intent, configures the lab, runs the experiment, and posts a live chart of the results in the conversation.

Both examples are in the Biology group of the Examples menu. Open one, open the chat, and type the prompts below.

Be patient. Every picosecond is computed in your browser. A pressure ladder on 200 rods takes many minutes, and the two-nanosecond hold of the last prompt is the longest experiment here.

Virus Particles That Order by Crowding

  1. Crowding — "Compress the rods at 300 K from 2 to 30 MPa, holding 260 ps at each pressure, and chart the nematic order against the concentration. Do they order by crowding?"
  2. Dilution — "Now let the pressure back down through the same steps. Does the order go away again?"

Worm-Like Chains That Order by Crowding

  1. The hinge — "Build a chain of 20 of these rods for each hinge stiffness from 0 to 6000 K, thermalize each at 300 K, and chart the persistence length against k_a/kT. Does the hinge set it the way the worm-like chain predicts?"
  2. Temperature — "Now heat the dimers in the lab from 300 to 600 K, about 400 ps at each step, and chart the persistence length of their hinge against temperature. Do they soften?"
  3. Crowding — "Compress the cell of dimers at 300 K from 1 to 12 MPa, holding at each pressure, and chart the nematic order against the rods' volume fraction. Do the hinged pairs align, and sooner than single rods would?"
  4. Stiff against floppy — "Repeat the compression with free joints, but as a fair test this time: bring a cell of the lab's dimers and a cell with a hinge of 0 K to the same volume fraction, 0.2, and hold both there for 2 ns. Do the floppy dimers still align?"

Crowding Alone Orders a Suspension

The first example holds 200 rods in a periodic cell at 300 K, dilute, the rods pointing every way. Onsager measured crowding by the concentration c = ρL²D, the number of rods per volume times the length squared times the width. His theory for infinitely thin hard rods keeps the suspension isotropic below c = 4.19 and makes it nematic above 5.34, with the two phases coexisting in between; he wrote those limits as 3.29 and 4.19 in his own unit of concentration, which carries a factor of π/4. For these rods, c = 4.19 means the rods fill 22 % of the cell and c = 5.34 means 28 %, a volume fraction inside the range that macromolecules fill in a cell. Asked to compress the rods from 2 to 30 MPa, holding 260 ps at each pressure, the agent used the Berendsen barostat, which rescales the cell a little every step until the rods push back with the set pressure, and charted the order parameter S against c. S is 1 when every rod points the same way and 0 when they point every way. At 2.1 MPa the cell is 97 Å across, c = 1.4 and S = 0.07, an isotropic suspension. The suspension stays isotropic to c = 3.9, at 14.3 MPa, where S = 0.16, and is nematic by c = 5.4, at 18.5 MPa, where S = 0.87; S crosses 0.5 at c = 4.7. At 30.5 MPa, c = 7.1 and S = 0.94, with the rods filling 37 % of a cell 56 Å across. The thermostat read 300 K throughout and the attraction between the rods stayed at 20 K, so the order came from the crowding, and the crossing at 4.7 falls inside the window between 4.19 and 5.34 in which Onsager's theory has the two phases coexisting, as it should for rods of aspect 10 with a soft contact. Each pressure is held 200 ps before the 60 ps of measurement, and long rods turn slowly, so the order at each step is a lower bound. Buttons under the chart put the cell at any pressure into the lab, where the Director arrow shows the common axis.

Let back down through the same pressures, the order went away again: S fell from 0.92 at c = 6.4 to 0.06 at c = 1.4, crossing 0.5 at c = 3.7, a lower concentration than the 4.7 at which it formed and below the 4.19 at which the theory has the isotropic phase alone. The gap is hysteresis, the mark of a first-order transition, widened here by rods that take time to turn, so that a 200 ps hold lags the equilibrium on both legs. Concentration alone made and unmade the nematic. This is why a suspension of tobacco mosaic virus turns birefringent as it is concentrated and clears again on dilution, and it is the mechanism by which crowding orders the long molecules of a cell: a dense bundle of filaments or a concentrated solution of DNA aligns for the same reason these rods do.

Crowding a Chain That Bends

Most of the long molecules of a cell are chains that bend. The length over which a chain keeps its direction against thermal kicks is its persistence length: about 50 nm for DNA, as Hagerman reviewed in 1988, 17 µm for an actin filament, and millimeters for a microtubule. The model for such a chain is the worm-like chain of Kratky and Porod, a line that resists bending. A chain of Gay–Berne rods hinged tip to tip is the shortest worm there is: each hinge costs an energy ka(1 − cos θ) to bend by an angle θ, and that one number sets the stiffness of the whole chain. The second example starts from the virus rods of the first, joined in a hundred pairs by a hinge of 6000 K, twenty times the thermal energy at room temperature, in a periodic cell dilute enough that the pairs rarely meet. A pair is a rod of aspect 20 that can bend at its middle. Flory's question of 1956, worked out for semiflexible chains by Khokhlov and Semenov in 1981, is whether a crowded chain orders at a concentration set by its full length or only by the length over which it is straight.

The first two chat turns establish what the hinge does. The agent built a fresh chain of 20 rods for each of eight hinge stiffnesses, from a free joint to 6000 K, held each at 300 K, and measured the average of cos θ between neighboring rods, four chains per stiffness. The persistence length follows from that average, ℓp = −b/ln⟨cos θ⟩ with b the length of a rod, and statistical mechanics predicts the average: for a hinge that costs ka(1 − cos θ), ⟨cos θ⟩ = coth x − 1/x with x = ka/kT, the Langevin function that also gives the magnetization of a paramagnet. The measured persistence length climbed from 0.4 rods at the free joint to 19.0 rods (756 Å) at 6000 K, and the prediction, drawn as a line with no fitted parameter, ran through the points. Heated from 300 to 600 K, the hundred pairs in the lab softened from 19.4 to 9.5 rods of persistence, against 19.5 and 9.5 predicted, with the hinge unchanged, because x fell from 20 to 10. At 300 K a pair with the 6000 K hinge is nearly straight, its joint swinging about 18°, and a pair with a free joint folds to any angle. Those are the two objects the crowding turns compare.

The third turn compressed the cell of stiff pairs at 300 K on a pressure ladder, from 1 to 12 MPa, holding 200 ps at each pressure before 60 ps of measurement, and charted S against the fraction φ of the cell the rods fill. The pairs stayed a tangle while they had room, S between 0.08 and 0.15 up to φ = 0.18, and then aligned: S crossed 0.5 at φ = 0.23 and reached 0.98 at φ = 0.47, in a cell 52 Å across. The single rods of the first example crossed at φ = 0.24, so on this ladder the pairs ordered at about the same concentration as the rods they are made of. Onsager's threshold falls as 1/L² in number density, so a rigid rod twice as long should order at about half the volume fraction, near φ = 0.11 for a straight 80 Å rod, and the ladder put the pairs far above that. The reason is time. A pair of 40 Å rods needs about a nanosecond at one density to find the common axis, and each pressure on the ladder is held a fifth of that, so the ladder passes the concentration at which the pairs would order before they have ordered, and the crossing it records is late. The chat says so, and the fourth turn gives the pairs the time.

The fourth turn makes the test fair by holding one concentration. The agent built two fresh cells of a hundred pairs, one with the 6000 K hinge and one with free joints, brought each to the same volume fraction, φ = 0.20, the rods filling a fifth of a cell 69 Å across, in 200 ps, and then held both there for two nanoseconds with the volume fixed, sampling S every 20 ps. The stiff pairs started as a tangle, S = 0.16, and ordered on their own: S rose through 0.5 after 360 ps and reached 0.86 by the end, a nematic with the Director arrow to show for it. The free-jointed pairs stayed a tangle for the whole two nanoseconds: S = 0.07 at the end, and their joints were only slightly straightened by the crowd, ⟨cos θ⟩ 0.36 at the start and 0.35 at the end, against 0.96 for the stiff hinge. The two cells have the same rods at the same concentration and the same temperature; the only difference between them is the hinge. A straight pair is a rod of aspect 20 and orders where Onsager says such a rod should, below the single rods' 0.24. A pair that folds is two rods of aspect 10 that happen to be tied together, with the excluded volume of the two and little more alignment to gain than the single rods, which at φ = 0.20 are still isotropic. This is Flory's rule for polymer liquid crystals: a chain's length counts toward the nematic only as far as it is straight, so the stiffer the chain, the lower the concentration at which it orders. It is the reason liquid-crystal polymers such as Kevlar are built from rigid units, and the reason DNA, with its 50 nm persistence length, forms liquid crystals at concentrations where a flexible polymer of the same mass is still an entangled melt. Buttons under the chart put the stiff cell at any time along the hold into the lab, and Load this state on the card puts the free-jointed cell there.

What a Cell Does with This

The volume fraction at which these rods and pairs order, about a fifth to a quarter of the cell, is the volume fraction macromolecules fill in a living cell, and a cell uses the effect in both directions. Where long stiff molecules are packed densely, as DNA is inside a bacteriophage capsid or actin is in a bundle, they align, and the alignment costs the cell no energy because entropy provides it. A chain wound into coils, like the free-jointed pairs here, gains little by lining up, so coiling a long molecule is a way to keep it disordered in a crowd. Confinement does what crowding does by another route: the companion article shows the same virus rods, at a concentration too low to order by crowding, lining up along a box as it is narrowed at fixed volume, the way filaments point along a narrow cell. A companion article, Order from Entropy: Filaments in a Crowd, follows the same rods among a crowd of small spheres, which line them up and then separate from them. In the lab a student can test any of these with a question: ask the agent to hold the dimers at another volume fraction, set the hinge to any stiffness in the bead table and hold them again, or build longer chains and crowd those.

What the Simulations Produced

The simulations were given the shape of a rod, a 20 K attraction, and the hinge that joins two rods. Everything else below came out of them:

  • Order from crowding — 200 nearly hard rods of aspect 10, compressed at 300 K, stay isotropic to c = 3.9 and are nematic by c = 5.4, crossing S = 0.5 at c = 4.7, inside Onsager’s coexistence window of 4.19 to 5.34.
  • Hysteresis on dilution — expanded through the same pressures, the order melts at c = 3.7, below the 4.7 at which it formed.
  • The worm-like chain's law — chains of 20 rods at 300 K have a persistence length of 0.4 rods at a free joint and 19.0 rods (756 Å) at a 6000 K hinge, on the Langevin prediction coth x − 1/x with no fitted parameter, and soften from 19.4 to 9.5 rods between 300 and 600 K.
  • Chains that order by crowding — compressed on a pressure ladder at 300 K, the stiff pairs stay disordered to φ = 0.18, cross S = 0.5 at φ = 0.23 and reach S = 0.98 at φ = 0.47.
  • A late crossing explained — the pairs cross near the single rods' φ = 0.24 on the ladder because a pair needs about a nanosecond at one density to order and each pressure is held 200 ps.
  • Flory's rule — held at φ = 0.20 for two nanoseconds, the pairs with a 6000 K hinge order within 360 ps and reach S = 0.86, and the pairs with free joints end at S = 0.07 with their joints at ⟨cos θ⟩ = 0.35.

Conclusion

This article uses molecular crowding to illustrate a new way to learn science: students converse with an AI agent that turns a plain-language question into a molecular dynamics experiment and returns measured data. A suspension of virus-like rods that orders when it is crowded and clears when it is diluted, and hinged chains whose stiffness decides whether the crowd aligns them, become something students measure for themselves in a browser. By pairing a physically grounded coarse-grained simulation with an AI that can set it up and run it, AIMS turns a student's curiosity into a genuine scientific investigation of how the packed interior of a cell organizes the molecules in it.
