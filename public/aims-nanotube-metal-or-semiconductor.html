Carbon Nanotubes: Metal or Semiconductor?

Roll a sheet of graphene one way and the nanotube conducts like a metal. Roll it a slightly different way and it is a semiconductor. Ask an AI agent in AIMS to build the nanotubes atom by atom and compute which is which.

By Charles Xie ✉

A carbon nanotube is a sheet of graphene rolled into a cylinder about a nanometer across. Iijima reported the first ones in 1991. Within a year, Hamada, Sawada and Oshiyama, and independently Saito, Fujita, Dresselhaus and Dresselhaus, predicted from band theory that the way the sheet is rolled decides whether the nanotube is a metal or a semiconductor. The roll is described by two integers (n, m) that say how many hexagons the circumference spans along the two directions of the honeycomb lattice. (n, 0) nanotubes are called zigzag and (n, n) nanotubes armchair, after the pattern of their rims. The prediction was that a nanotube is metallic when n − m is a multiple of three and semiconducting otherwise, so that one nanotube in three, taken at random, is a metal. In 1998 Wildöer and coworkers and Odom and coworkers confirmed the rule on individual nanotubes with a scanning tunneling microscope. The gap of a semiconducting nanotube is inversely proportional to its diameter.

The rule matters because the two kinds of nanotube come out of the same furnace together. A transistor needs a semiconducting channel, and Tans, Verschueren and Dekker built the first nanotube transistor in 1998 from a single semiconducting nanotube. A wire needs a metallic one. Sorting the two kinds, or growing only one of them, has been a central problem of nanotube electronics ever since.

The usual textbook treatment is a picture of the graphene Brillouin zone with lines drawn across it. In AIMS a student can test the rule on nanotubes they choose. They name a nanotube by its two integers, the AI agent builds it atom by atom, solves its electronic structure in a tight-binding model, and posts the band structure in the chat. The example below has three turns: build a zigzag nanotube and classify it, build an armchair nanotube and compare, then sweep ten zigzag nanotubes to find which are metallic and how the gap depends on the diameter.

Why the Rolling Direction Decides

Graphene is a semiconductor with a gap of zero. Its valence band and conduction band touch at two points of the Brillouin zone, called K and K′, and are separated everywhere else. The electrons that carry the current are the ones in the pz orbitals, one per carbon atom, standing perpendicular to the sheet. In the simplest model of graphene each electron hops between neighboring pz orbitals with an energy of about 2.7 eV, and that single hopping energy produces the two bands and their touching points. This is the π tight-binding model, and it is the model the agent solves.

Rolling the sheet into a nanotube adds one condition. An electron wave traveling around the circumference must return to its starting value after one lap, so only wavelengths that fit a whole number of times around the nanotube are allowed. In the Brillouin zone of graphene this restricts the electron to a set of parallel lines, one for each allowed wavelength, and the integers (n, m) fix where the lines fall. When one of the lines passes through a K point, the nanotube inherits the touching bands of graphene and is a metal. When every line misses, the bands closest to K are separated by a gap, and the nanotube is a semiconductor. Whether a line passes through K depends on n − m. If n − m is a multiple of three, a line passes through. Otherwise the nearest line misses K by one third of the line spacing, and the gap is set by that distance. The line spacing is inversely proportional to the circumference, so the gap is inversely proportional to the diameter d. In the π model the gap of a semiconducting nanotube is 2·t·acc/d, where t is the hopping energy and acc the carbon–carbon bond length of 0.142 nm. With t = 2.7 eV, that is 0.77 eV·nm divided by the diameter. This way of obtaining the bands of a nanotube from the bands of graphene is called zone folding.

A Zigzag Nanotube and an Armchair Nanotube

In the first turn the agent builds a (10,0) zigzag nanotube, 0.78 nm across and 2.13 nm long, 200 carbon atoms. It loads the segment into the lab and solves the infinitely long nanotube by repeating one period along the axis. The band-structure chart shows a gap of 0.95 eV at the center of the zone, and 10 − 0 leaves a remainder of 1 on division by three, so the nanotube is a semiconductor. In the second turn the agent builds a (6,6) armchair nanotube of the same length, 0.81 nm across and 192 atoms. Its band-structure chart shows two bands crossing at the Fermi level, two thirds of the way from the zone center to the zone boundary, and no gap. The two nanotubes have nearly the same diameter and nearly the same number of atoms per nanometer of length. They differ in the direction in which the sheet was rolled.

Each turn also posts an energy-level chart for the 2 nm segment the lab holds, and the segment behaves differently from the infinite nanotube. The (10,0) segment has fourteen levels within 0.2 eV of zero, seven at each end, in the middle of what should be a 0.95 eV gap. They live on the two open rims, where every carbon atom has lost one of its three bonds. A zigzag edge of graphene carries a band of such states pinned at the Fermi level, as Nakada, Fujita, Dresselhaus and Dresselhaus showed in 1996, and a zigzag nanotube cut open has one such edge at each end. The agent tells the two kinds of level apart by where each wavefunction lives, and the chart draws the rim states dashed. The segment's own extended states are 2.3 eV apart, far more than the 0.95 eV of the infinite nanotube, because a 2 nm box quantizes them. A real nanotube is capped or chemically terminated, and the rim states are gone. The number that classifies the nanotube is the gap of the infinite one. The (6,6) segment shows a second finite-size effect. Its highest occupied and lowest empty levels are 0.59 eV apart, although the infinite nanotube is a metal, because a 2 nm piece of any metal has discrete levels. Armchair rims carry no edge states, so its level chart has no dashed lines.

Ten Zigzag Nanotubes

The third turn builds every zigzag nanotube from (7,0) to (16,0), solves each one, and plots the band gap against the diameter. Three of the ten have no gap, and they are the three whose n is divisible by three. The other seven have gaps that fall as the nanotube widens, from 1.33 eV at 0.55 nm to 0.60 eV at 1.25 nm, and the chart draws the zone-folding line 0.77 eV·nm/d through them. Every semiconducting point lies within five percent of the line.

The gaps alternate around the line because the nearest allowed line misses the K point on one side for (7,0), (10,0), (13,0) and (16,0) and on the other side for (8,0), (11,0) and (14,0), and the energy surface of graphene is a cone only close to K. Farther out it is warped into a triangle, so a line passing on one side of K gives a slightly smaller gap than a line at the same distance on the other side. A sweep of armchair nanotubes would put every point at zero, since n − m is zero for all of them.

The three turns of the example

  1. The zigzag nanotube — "Build a (10,0) carbon nanotube about 2 nm long and tell me whether it conducts like a metal or a semiconductor."
  2. The armchair nanotube — "Now build a (6,6) armchair tube of the same length and compare the two."
  3. The sweep — "Sweep the zigzag tubes from (7,0) to (16,0): how does the band gap depend on the diameter, and which ones are metallic?"

Go further

  1. A chiral nanotube — "Build a (6,5) chiral tube. Is it metallic?" Here n − m is 1. The period of a chiral nanotube is much longer than that of a zigzag or armchair one, so this one has hundreds of atoms per period.
  2. The armchair family — "Sweep the armchair tubes from (5,5) to (10,10)." Every point should be at zero.
  3. Ribbons — "Make a 9-atom-wide armchair graphene nanoribbon and compare its gap with a 10-wide one." Armchair ribbons come in three families by width, for the same reason nanotubes do.
  4. A closed cage — "Build C60 and show me its energy levels." Buckminsterfullerene has sixty carbon atoms and no rim, and the same π model gives it a gap of 2.0 eV between its highest occupied and lowest empty levels.

The Model and Its Limits

The agent builds each nanotube by geometry alone, from the two integers and the carbon–carbon bond length. The electronic structure comes from the nearest-neighbor π tight-binding model with a hopping energy of 2.7 eV, which is the model in which the (n, m) rule was first derived. The infinite nanotube is solved by applying Bloch's theorem along the axis to one period of the structure. The finite segment in the lab is solved as a cluster, every pz orbital at once. The segment also carries the Tersoff carbon force field, so it can be heated, stretched or cut in the lab. The force field governs the motion of the atoms, and the tight-binding model governs the electrons.

The results are properties of this model. The model treats the bonds of a rolled sheet exactly as it treats the bonds of a flat one. In a real nanotube the curvature mixes the in-plane σ orbitals into the π states, and for the nominally metallic zigzag nanotubes that mixing opens a small gap. Ouyang, Huang, Cheung and Lieber measured those gaps by tunneling spectroscopy in 2001 and found them to shrink as the inverse square of the nanotube radius. Armchair nanotubes have no such gap, because their symmetry protects the crossing. The (9,0), (12,0) and (15,0) nanotubes in the table are therefore semiconductors with very small gaps where the model says zero. The same mixing lowers the gaps of the narrowest semiconducting nanotubes below the π-model values, so the (7,0) and (8,0) entries in the table are the least reliable. The model also omits the interaction between electrons, which matters for the ribbons in the Go further list. The hopping energy and the bond length are the two quantities in the model taken from experiment, and the tunneling measurements of 1998 gave the inverse-diameter law with a hopping energy close to the one used here.

What the Solver Produced

The model was given the positions of the carbon atoms and one hopping energy. Everything below came out of the calculation:

  • The rule — among the ten zigzag nanotubes, exactly (9,0), (12,0) and (15,0) have no gap, and (6,6) has none either.
  • The inverse-diameter law — the seven semiconducting gaps fall within five percent of 0.77 eV·nm divided by the diameter.
  • The rim states — the open (10,0) segment has fourteen levels near the Fermi energy, seven at each end, and the agent located them on the rims before classifying the nanotube by its infinite-length gap.
  • Two nanotubes, one difference — the (10,0) and (6,6) nanotubes have nearly the same diameter and atom count, and one is a semiconductor with a 0.95 eV gap while the other is a metal.

Conclusion

This article uses the electronic structure of carbon nanotubes to illustrate a new way to learn science: students converse with an AI agent that turns a plain-language question into a quantum mechanical calculation and hands back the result as a chart. The rule that a nanotube is a metal when n − m is a multiple of three, normally a sentence in a nanoscience course, becomes something students test for themselves. They build a zigzag nanotube and an armchair nanotube of the same size, find a gap in one and none in the other, and sweep a family of ten to watch the gap fall with the diameter and vanish at every third member. They also learn how a finite segment differs from an infinite nanotube, and where the model departs from the measured nanotubes. By pairing a physically grounded solver with an AI that can build the structures and run it, AIMS lets students investigate a rule they would otherwise only read about.
