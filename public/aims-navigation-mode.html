Fantastic Voyage in AIMS: Navigating in the World of Atoms and Molecules

Inspired by the 1966 film, AIMS' navigation mode lets users fly a spaceship through the molecular world, scan atoms on the way, take guided tours planned by an AI agent, and carry molecules into binding sites and through channels.

By Charles Xie ✉ · Updated November 11, 2026

Gamification is a strategy to engage and entice users by mimicking gaming experience. A goal of our AIMS platform is to create a serious game that can stimulate users' interest in exploring the world of atoms and molecules. We envision such a user interface to deliver immersive experiences for users to "witness" how atoms and molecules interact with one another to create the wonders that we see in the familiar macroscopic world. Our work was inspired by Fantastic Voyage — a 1966 Oscar-winning science fiction film about a submarine and its crew shrunk to a microscopic size and venture into the body of an injured scientist to save his life.

The navigation mode of AIMS puts you in a spaceship about the size of a water molecule. The camera is the ship: you fly it through the scene with the keyboard, a gamepad or, on a touch screen, two thumbsticks, and a head-up display and a dashboard tell you where you are, how fast you are moving and which atom you are looking at. The window below shows a hemoglobin molecule with a small molecule placed in one of its binding pockets. Click the window and fly in.

Flying the Ship

Navigation mode works in every type of AIMS project. Press Ctrl+U (⌘+U on a Mac) or choose Navigation View in the menu to enter or leave it. Like in other games, AIMS uses the WASD keys to move: W and S fly forward and backward, A and D move left and right, and Z and X move up and down. The arrow keys turn the ship (pitch and yaw), and Q and E roll it. The ship has momentum: it speeds up while a key is held and coasts to a stop after the key is released. At the default thrust it covers about 15 Å per second, so crossing a small protein takes a few seconds. The Thrust slider on the dashboard scales that speed from a tenth to five times, and holding Shift slows the ship to a quarter speed for fine maneuvers. The buttons on the dashboard do the same as the keys while you hold them down. For a harder ride, check Newtonian flight in the menu at the right end of the dashboard. The ship then keeps moving in the same direction when it turns, and it slows down only gradually unless you thrust against its motion.

You can fly from the cockpit, with the frame of the windshield around the view, or from behind the ship, with the ship in front of you. A button on the dashboard switches between the two views. Atoms are solid: when the ship comes within about 1.6 Å of an atom, it slides along the atom and stops, the screen flashes red and the ship shakes. The menu at the right end of the dashboard has three fields for jumping straight to a set of coordinates.

Reading the Instruments

A reticle in the middle of the screen marks the line of flight. When it points at an atom, it turns amber and names the atom, the molecule the atom belongs to and its distance. Clicking an atom locks the reticle on it. Press P, or click the atom, to open a scanner card: the card lists the element, its mass and partial charge, the atoms it is bonded to with the bond lengths, and how many atoms are packed around it. The card has a button that copies the scan into the chat, where it stays as a record of what you found, and a button that flies the ship to the atom.

The dashboard along the bottom carries the rest of the instruments. A bearing dial shows where the target of the autopilot, the selected molecule or the center of the scene is relative to the nose of the ship: a dot at the center means straight ahead, on the middle ring to one side, at the rim behind you. A speed tape shows how fast you are flying. A radar shows the atoms around the ship from above, with the forward direction up. A ring fills as the nearest atom gets closer and turns red on contact. When the molecule is out of sight, an arrow at the edge of the screen points back to it.

The ship can also be heard. Sound is off by default, and the speaker button on the dashboard turns it on. Within about 5 Å of an atom the ship beeps, faster and higher in pitch as it gets closer, and it makes a thud when it touches an atom.

Flying with an AI Agent

Press F to fly to the selected atom or molecule, or to the center of the scene when nothing is selected. An autopilot turns the ship, flies it there and parks it next to the target, steering around atoms on the way. The AI agent of AIMS can fly the ship too. Ask it to "fly me into the nanotube" or to "give me a tour of the water molecules", and it plans the route, flies it, and pauses at each stop with a line of narration at the top of the screen. The tour is saved in the chat as a card that lists the stops. Clicking a stop flies the ship back there, and Replay flies the whole tour again.

The window below shows water molecules in a carbon nanotube. Its chat holds two turns: the agent flies the ship into the nanotube, and then takes it on a tour of the ten water molecules in the order they sit along the channel. Press Replay in the tour card to take the tour, or run the simulation and fly down the channel to watch the water molecules move.

Riding Along with a Molecule

In a running simulation the molecules move, and a molecule that you want to watch soon leaves the view. Click one of its atoms and press G, or the ride button on the dashboard, and the ship moves with whatever moves with that atom: a small molecule as a whole, one chain of a polymer, or one nanoparticle of a collection of nanoparticles. The ship follows the position of the molecule and keeps its own heading. A molecule in a liquid or a gas changes direction at every collision, so a camera that turned with it would spin wildly. While riding, you can still turn the ship and move around the molecule to look at it from another side. Press G again to get off.

Carrying a Molecule

In a Drug Discovery project, the ship can carry the ligand into the binding site of the protein, the way a pilot docks it by hand. Press T to pick up the ligand. It rides in front of the nose of the ship, see-through so that the protein shows behind it, and a readout above the dashboard shows its docking score, its hydrogen bonds with the protein and its clashes with the protein as you fly. Green dashed lines mark the hydrogen bonds and red ones the clashes, and with sound on, a chime marks each new hydrogen bond. Press R to turn the ligand with the arrow keys and Q and E, and press R again to steer the ship. The protein is a wall: the ligand stops at its surface, WALL lights up in the readout, and the ship cannot pull away from the ligand. Back up and steer, or turn the ligand, to get past.

When the ligand sits in the binding site, press L to lock it in. AIMS relaxes the pose downhill on the docking score and shows your score next to the relaxed one. Then you can run Search to see whether the automatic docking finds a better pose. Press T to let go of the ligand at any time, and Ctrl+Z to undo a tow. The AI agent can tow, turn, let go of and lock in the ligand too.

In the example Docking Biotin into Streptavidin by Hand, the ship waits outside one of the four binding pockets of streptavidin, with biotin in front of it. Streptavidin binds biotin more tightly than almost any other protein binds a small molecule. Biotin locked in close to the pose found in the crystal structure relaxes to a docking score of about −2.1 eV with five hydrogen bonds. In our test, Search stopped at the rim of the pocket at about −1.5 eV, so a careful pilot can beat the automatic docking.

The same controls carry a molecule through a channel. Aquaporins are the water channels of cell membranes, and Peter Agre shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for discovering the first one, aquaporin-1. Each of its four subunits has its own pore, so narrow that water molecules pass through it in single file. At the narrowest point, the selectivity filter, four side chains pinch the pore to about 2.8 Å, the width of one water molecule.

The window below opens above one pore of aquaporin-1, looking down into it. Its chat holds two turns. In the first, the agent takes the ship on a tour down the pore with five stops, from the outside of the cell to the inside. In the second, it explains how to carry a water molecule through the pore yourself. The pale blue spheres are the water molecules found in the crystal structure. When you let go of the water molecule, a Tow Profile card in the chat plots its energy and its hydrogen bonds along the pore. Then pick glycerol or urea in the gallery and fly it down the same way. Water passes the selectivity filter and makes up to four hydrogen bonds there. Glycerol stops where the outer vestibule narrows, about 15 Å above the filter. Each new Tow Profile card draws the earlier tows behind it, so the glycerol curve ends where the wall held the glycerol while the water curve runs through the pore.
