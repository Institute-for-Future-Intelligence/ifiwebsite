AIMS

Quantitative Structure–Property Relationships of Acenes

Acenes — linear fusions of benzene rings — show boiling points that rise with carbon count. AIMS lets you build QSPR models and run molecular dynamics to probe why.

← Back to AIMS

By Charles Xie ✉ · Updated July 11, 2026

Acenes are a family of aromatic hydrocarbon molecules, most of which are polycyclic, as opposed to monocyclic molecules such as cycloalkanes. They are formed by joining benzene rings in a linear way, following the general formula C4n+2H2n+4. As with the linear alkanes and cycloalkanes, their boiling points increase with the number of carbon atoms. With AIMS, you can study this quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR). In general, a good QSPR model must meet the so-called OECD principles, which require the model to have 1) a defined endpoint; 2) an unambiguous algorithm; 3) a defined domain of applicability; 4) appropriate measures of goodness-of-fit, robustness, and predictivity; and 5) a mechanistic interpretation, if possible.

You can follow the instructions in our article about linear alkanes to build a regression QSPR model for acenes using a training set and then examine its validity with a test set. It should not be difficult to develop a QSPR model that meets the first four OECD principles. Keep in mind that the boiling points of the larger acenes are computed estimates rather than measurements, because acenes beyond pentacene are too reactive to be isolated in bulk. A model trained on them is therefore partly fitting the output of another model.

QSPR modeling gives us a tool to analyze the data, but it does not provide any explanation about the result by itself. To make sense of the result, we still need to resort to fundamentals in chemistry. Equipped with the power of molecular dynamics simulations, AIMS allows us to design and conduct computational experiments to check a QSPR model and/or find a theoretical explanation (the fifth OECD principle).

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

The following two simulations allow you to compare the boiling points of benzene and octacene on a qualitative basis.

Benzene (C6H6)

There are 13 benzene molecules (156 atoms in total) in this simulation. The temperature is initially set to be 373 K (100°C). A greatly exaggerated gravitational field is applied to keep the molecules at the bottom of the container when they condense. As you can see, at this temperature, benzene is in the gaseous state. The atoms are colored by their kinetic energy — blue represents low kinetic energy and red high kinetic energy. The dashed lines represent the van der Waals interactions among the atoms.

Note that it may take a while for a molecular dynamics simulation to produce a desired emergent behavior (e.g., condensation), especially on slower computers, when you change a simulation parameter such as lowering the temperature.

Octacene (C34H20)

There are three octacene molecules (162 atoms in total) in this simulation. The temperature is initially set to be 373 K (100°C). As with benzene above, a greatly exaggerated gravitational field is applied to keep the molecules near the bottom of the container when they condense. As you can see, at this temperature, the octacene molecules are in a condensed state occupying the lower part of the container. If you drag the mouse to rotate the container and let it stay in a certain orientation, you can observe that these molecules will start falling to the bottom of the container, but they manage to stick together while moving down. This is not the case with the benzene molecules in the above simulation.

These simulations do not predict the boiling points of benzene and octacene (80°C from experiments and an estimated 745°C, respectively — octacene is too reactive to be isolated in bulk, so its boiling point has only been computed). What they show is that, at 373 K, benzene is a gas and octacene is condensed, which places the boiling point of benzene below 373 K and that of octacene above it. With only a handful of molecules and an exaggerated gravitational field, the picture is qualitative. Octacene has the higher boiling point because its larger molecules have more contact area with one another and therefore stronger total van der Waals attraction to keep them together, as indicated by the dashed lines shown in the above windows. The rotation experiment above shows this attraction at work: the octacene molecules stick together as they fall, whereas the benzene molecules do not.

Compared with linear alkanes and cycloalkanes, the planarity of acenes allows for more efficient stacking between molecules in the solid or liquid state, thereby increasing the strength of their intermolecular forces and making their boiling points higher than those of molecules of similar weight from the other two families of hydrocarbons. For example, naphthalene (C10H8) has a boiling point of 218°C, while nonane (C9H20) has a boiling point of 151°C. Their molecular weights are both 128 g/mol. As an exercise, think about how you would turn molecular planarity into a number that a QSPR model could use.
